### Recording tips
- **Tour clips are timed.** Each line has a time range that matches when the caption and camera change. Start the line when the time
  starts. If you finish early, pause and smile. Don't squeeze in extra words.
- **Stop-page clips are not timed.** Talk like you're proudly showing a guest around your own spa.
- No "Hi, I'm ___". Every clip starts right on the content.
- Words to say carefully: **pharynx** (FAIR-inks), **larynx** (LAIR-inks), **conchae** (KON-kee), **epiglottis** (ep-ih-GLOT-iss),
  **bronchi** (BRON-kye), **bronchioles** (BRON-kee-ohls), **alveoli** (al-VEE-oh-lye), **surfactant** (sur-FAK-tunt),
  **medulla oblongata** (meh-DULL-uh ob-long-GAH-tuh), **intercostal** (in-ter-KOS-tul), **hemoglobin** (HEE-moh-gloh-bin),
  **emphysema** (em-fih-SEE-muh).

## Stop pages

### Stop 1 · Nasal cavity · The Nasal Welcome Lodge · Tawfic

> This is stop one, the nasal cavity. We call it the Nasal Welcome Lodge, because every breath checks in here first.
>
> Air comes in through the nostrils into the nasal cavity. Before we let it go any deeper, our lodge does three jobs: it cleans
> the air, warms it, and moistens it.
>
> The curvy shelves on the side walls are the nasal conchae, our Heated Rainforest Walls. Their shape makes the air swirl, so more
> of it touches the warm, wet lining. That's how the structure fits the function.
>
> The lining is covered in sticky mucus that traps dust and germs. Tiny hair-like cilia sweep that mucus back to the pharynx,
> where it's swallowed. That's our housekeeping team, and they never take a day off. Up at the very top are the smell receptors,
> our Aromatherapy Suite.
>
> Try the diagram. Switch between breathing through your nose and breathing through your mouth. Mouth breathing skips all three
> jobs, so the air arrives cold, dry and dirty. That's why we always recommend the nose entrance.
>
> We hope you enjoyed our lodge. If you'd like to stay, our Day Breather package starts right here.

### Stop 2 · Pharynx · Crossroads Plaza · Tawfic

> Stop two is the pharynx, our Crossroads Plaza. It's where the air route and the food route cross.
>
> The pharynx is the tube behind the nasal cavity and the mouth. It has three regions, like three floors. The nasopharynx is
> behind the nasal cavity, and only air goes through it. The oropharynx is behind the mouth, so air and food both pass here. And
> the laryngopharynx is the bottom floor, right above the larynx.
>
> The tonsils are our security guards. They're lymph tissue that traps pathogens as they come in, so our guests stay safe.
>
> At the bottom is the Great Split. Air goes forward into the larynx. Food goes back into the esophagus.
>
> Try the diagram. Switch between breathe and swallow. When you swallow, the soft palate closes off the nasal cavity, and the
> epiglottis covers the airway. That's why you can't breathe and swallow at the same time.
>
> We hope you liked the plaza. It's included in every package, so book your stay and see it for yourself.

### Stop 3 · Larynx · The Voice Box Concert Hall · Tawfic

> Stop three is the larynx, also called the voice box. We built a concert hall here, with a drawbridge out front.
>
> The larynx sits on top of the trachea. Its walls are made of cartilage, which is stiff, so the airway always stays open. The
> biggest piece is the thyroid cartilage, which forms the Adam's apple.
>
> The drawbridge is the epiglottis. It's a flap that folds down over the larynx every time you swallow, so food doesn't go into the
> trachea. If it doesn't close in time, food goes "down the wrong way", and you cough to push it back out.
>
> Inside is our main stage: the vocal cords. They're two folds of tissue. When you breathe out, air passes between them and makes
> them vibrate. That vibration is your voice, which is why you only speak when you exhale.
>
> Try the diagram. Switch between breathing and speaking. When you breathe, the vocal cords are wide open. When you speak, they
> close and vibrate.
>
> We hope you enjoyed the show. The concert hall is the last stop on our Day Breather package. Book now and save your seat.

### Stop 4 · Trachea · The Trachea Express · Oliver

> Stop four is the trachea. We call it the Trachea Express, a tunnel straight down to the lungs.
>
> The trachea carries air from the larynx down into the chest. It's about ten to twelve centimetres long.
>
> Its walls have sixteen to twenty C-shaped rings of cartilage. Those are our rail tunnels. The cartilage holds the trachea open,
> so it never collapses when you breathe in. The open part of the C faces backwards, toward the esophagus. That gap lets the
> esophagus stretch when you swallow. So one shape does two jobs.
>
> The lining is our luggage return. Goblet cells make mucus, which traps dust. Cilia sweep it up toward the pharynx, like an
> escalator, so it never reaches your lungs.
>
> Remember, the trachea only moves air. No gas exchange happens here.
>
> Try the diagram. Switch between a healthy trachea and a smoker's trachea. Smoking stops the cilia from sweeping, so mucus builds
> up. That's what causes smoker's cough, and it's why our spa is smoke-free.
>
> We hope you enjoyed the ride. All aboard the Trachea Express with our Deep Dive package.

### Stop 5 · Bronchi · The Grand Fork · Oliver

> Stop five is the bronchi, our Grand Fork. This is where one tunnel becomes two, one for each lung.
>
> At the bottom of the trachea, the airway splits into the right and left bronchi. The right bronchus is wider and straighter
> than the left. That's why, if someone breathes in a small object, it usually ends up in the right lung.
>
> The bronchi keep branching into smaller and smaller tubes. Because it looks like an upside-down tree, it's called the bronchial
> tree. All that branching spreads the air through the whole lung.
>
> Each lung is divided into sections called lobes. These are our Lung Wings. The right lung has three lobes, and the left lung has
> only two, because the heart sits slightly to the left and needs room.
>
> Try the diagram. Switch between airways and lobes to see the branches and the three-plus-two lobes. Fun fact: the spot where
> the trachea splits is extra sensitive. If anything touches it, you cough hard.
>
> We hope you enjoyed the view from the Grand Fork. Book your stay and pick a wing.

### Stop 6 · Bronchioles · The Bronchiole Canopy Walk · Oliver

> Stop six is the bronchioles, our Bronchiole Canopy Walk. These are tiny trails with walls that can squeeze.
>
> Bronchioles are the smallest airways in the lungs, about one millimetre wide or less. Unlike the trachea and bronchi, they have
> no cartilage. Their walls are smooth muscle instead.
>
> Smooth muscle can squeeze to make the airway narrower, or relax to make it wider. That lets your body control how much air reaches
> each part of the lung. It works automatically. You don't control it on purpose.
>
> At the end of every trail are clusters of tiny air sacs, the alveoli. That's where our spa is.
>
> Try the diagram. Switch between normal and an asthma attack. During an asthma attack, the smooth muscle squeezes and extra mucus
> forms, so the airway gets much narrower and less air gets through. An asthma inhaler relaxes the muscle so the trail opens back
> up.
>
> We hope you enjoyed the walk. It's the last trail before our spa, so book now and keep going.

### Stop 7 · Alveoli · The Alveoli Hot Springs · Oliver

> Stop seven is the alveoli, the Alveoli Hot Springs. This is our headline attraction, and honestly, it's our favourite.
>
> Alveoli are tiny air sacs at the very end of the bronchioles. You have about four hundred and eighty million of them. Together,
> their surface area is about half a tennis court. That huge surface means a lot of oxygen can get into the blood at once.
>
> The walls of each alveolus are just one cell thick, so gases only travel a very short distance. Each one is wrapped in a net of
> tiny blood vessels called capillaries. Those are our hot spring pipes.
>
> Some cells in the alveoli make surfactant. It's a slippery liquid that stops the alveoli from collapsing when you breathe out.
> You'll find it at our Surfactant Bar.
>
> So the structure fits the job perfectly: huge surface area, plus very thin walls, equals fast gas exchange.
>
> Try the diagram. Turn surfactant on and off. Without surfactant, the alveoli collapse and gas exchange gets much harder.
>
> We hope you loved our hot springs. To spend the whole weekend here, book our Alveoli Spa Weekend.

### Stop 7b · Gas exchange · The Great Exchange Market · Max

> This is stop 7b, gas exchange, our Great Exchange Market. Oxygen checks in, and carbon dioxide checks out.
>
> Gases move by diffusion. That means they move from where there's more of them to where there's less, and it doesn't need any
> energy.
>
> There's more oxygen in the alveoli than in the blood, so oxygen diffuses across the thin wall and into the blood. Once it's in,
> it rides on hemoglobin, a protein in red blood cells. Each hemoglobin has four seats, so it can carry up to four oxygen. About
> ninety-eight and a half percent of oxygen travels this way. Those are our shuttle seats.
>
> Carbon dioxide does the opposite. There's more of it in the blood, because your cells make it as waste. So it diffuses out of
> the blood and into the alveoli, and then you breathe it out.
>
> Try the diagram. Switch between both gases, just oxygen, or just carbon dioxide, to watch each one move. Notice the blood: it
> arrives low in oxygen, shown in blue, and leaves full of oxygen, shown in red.
>
> We hope you enjoyed the market. It's part of our Alveoli Spa Weekend, so book now and grab a seat on the shuttle.

### Stop 8 · Diaphragm · The Diaphragm Drop Tower · Max

> Stop eight is the diaphragm, our Diaphragm Drop Tower. It's our thrill ride, and it's the muscle that powers every breath.
>
> The diaphragm is a dome-shaped muscle under the lungs. It works with the intercostal muscles, which are between the ribs. The
> lungs have no muscle of their own. They just follow the chest.
>
> When you breathe in, the diaphragm contracts and moves down. That's the drop. The intercostals pull the ribs up and out. The
> chest gets bigger, so the pressure inside drops, and air rushes in.
>
> When you breathe out, the muscles relax. The chest gets smaller, the pressure goes up, and air flows out. That's the gentle
> return.
>
> The physics behind our ride is Boyle's law: bigger space, lower pressure. Air always flows from high pressure to low pressure.
>
> Try the diagram. Switch between inhale and exhale and watch the diaphragm and ribs move. At rest, you take about twelve to
> eighteen breaths a minute. And hiccups? Those are little spasms of the diaphragm.
>
> We hope you enjoyed the drop. Ride it as many times as you like with our Alveoli Spa Weekend.

### Extra · Breathing control · The Concierge Desk · Max

> This is our Concierge Desk: the control of breathing. Your brain runs your breathing automatically, so you never have to think
> about it.
>
> Our head concierge is the medulla oblongata, in the brainstem. It sends nerve signals to the diaphragm and the intercostal
> muscles to set your breathing rhythm.
>
> To know how fast to go, it uses chemoreceptors. These are sensors that detect how much carbon dioxide is in the blood. Carbon
> dioxide is the main signal to breathe.
>
> When carbon dioxide rises, like during exercise, the medulla makes you breathe faster and deeper to get rid of it. That's our
> rush hour.
>
> Try the diagram. Change the blood carbon dioxide level from low, to normal, to high, and watch the breathing speed up. That's
> also why you can't hold your breath forever: carbon dioxide builds up until your brain makes you breathe again.
>
> We hope you enjoyed meeting our concierge. Every package comes with round-the-clock service, so book with confidence.

### Extra · Exhalation · Check-out · Max

> This is check-out: exhalation. Your route home is the same trip, in reverse.
>
> Carbon dioxide is the waste gas made by your cells. It was picked up in the alveoli, and now it travels back out: alveoli,
> bronchioles, bronchi, trachea, larynx, pharynx, and out through the nose.
>
> Normal breathing out is passive. That means the muscles just relax. The diaphragm moves back up, the chest gets smaller, and air
> is pushed out. When you breathe out hard, like blowing out candles, the abdominal and intercostal muscles help push.
>
> Every guest leaves with a few souvenirs. Carbon dioxide, of course. Water vapour, which is why your breath fogs up a cold window.
> And your voice, because air going out is what makes the vocal cords vibrate.
>
> Try the diagram. It shows your whole route, so you can follow the trip home step by step.
>
> We hope you enjoyed your stay. Check-out is easy, and so is coming back. Book your next visit any time.

### Extra · Travel advisories · Respiratory disorders · Max

> These are our travel advisories: three conditions that can disrupt your stay. And a reminder: Alvea is a smoke-free and
> vape-free spa.
>
> First, asthma. It affects stops five and six. The airways get inflamed and the smooth muscle in the bronchioles squeezes, so
> less air gets through.
>
> Second, emphysema, a type of COPD. It affects stop seven. The walls of the alveoli break down, so there's much less surface area
> for gas exchange. The number one cause is smoking.
>
> Third, premature babies. This also affects stop seven. Babies born too early may not make enough surfactant yet, so their
> alveoli collapse and breathing is very hard.
>
> Try the diagram. Pick a condition and switch between healthy and affected to compare. Each one shows how a change in structure
> makes the function worse. Smoking and vaping damage the cilia, inflame the airways and break down the alveoli, so they're the
> biggest risk to the whole spa.
>
> We want every guest to have a healthy stay. Look after your lungs, and we'll see you soon.
